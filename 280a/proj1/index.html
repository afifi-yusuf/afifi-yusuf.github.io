Project 1 · Prokudin-Gorskii glass plates · Library of Congress · NumPy only

Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii collection

Between 1907 and 1915 Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii photographed the Russian Empire three times per scene, through a blue, a green and a red filter, onto one tall glass plate. He never got to project them in color. The Library of Congress scanned the plates, and this project puts the three exposures back on top of each other. The hard part is that nothing lines up: the plates shifted by up to 180 pixels between exposures, the emir's robe looks completely different in each filter, and the scans are 3700 pixels on a side. I wrote the alignment from scratch in NumPy, first as a brute-force search, then as a coarse-to-fine pyramid, then with gradients instead of pixels, and finished with automatic cropping, contrast, white balance and a fitted color matrix.

−15→15px

Single-scale alignment: try every shift, keep the best

Each scan is one tall grayscale image with the blue exposure on top, green in the middle and red at the bottom, so the first step is just cutting it into thirds. I convert to floats in [0, 1] whatever the file's bit depth (the JPEGs are 8-bit, the TIFFs 16-bit), take the blue plate as the fixed reference, and look for the translation of the green and the red plates that makes them look most like it.

At this size a brute-force search is fine. For every displacement (dy, dx) in a 31×31 window from −15 to +15 pixels I score how well the shifted plate matches the reference, and keep the best. The one thing that matters is what gets scored. The plates have black and white borders that do not belong to the picture, and a shifted plate leaves an empty strip along two edges, so I only score the interior: 10% is trimmed from every side of the reference, and the moving plate is compared on the same pixels through slicing rather than np.roll, so the wrapped-around strip is never seen. Without that trim the borders dominate the score and the search happily aligns the frames instead of the photograph.

I implemented four scores. L2 is the root mean squared difference of the two plates (smaller is better, so I negate it). NCC subtracts each plate's mean, normalizes to unit length and takes the dot product, which makes it indifferent to the fact that a red filter and a blue filter simply let through different amounts of light. SSIM and LPIPS are my two extra ones and are described in the metrics section. On the three small plates all four land on the same answer.

NCC = ⟨â, b̂⟩

where â = (a − mean a) / ‖a − mean a‖ and likewise for b. A shift is scored on the overlapping interior only, and the search takes the argmax over the 31×31 window.

1/32→1scale

Coarse to fine: an image pyramid for the big plates

The TIFF scans are about 3700 × 3200 pixels per plate and the plates are misaligned by up to 180 pixels, so the single-scale search would need a window of a few hundred pixels on a side, tens of thousands of shifts, each one a pass over 12 million pixels. Instead I search at a scale where the offset is small, then refine.

The pyramid is built by hand: each level is the previous one with every 2×2 block averaged into one pixel. The averaging is the anti-aliasing filter, and I stop before the short side drops below 64 pixels, which gives six levels for these scans (full size down to 1/32). At the coarsest level, roughly 100 × 115 pixels, I run the same exhaustive ±15 search as before, which at that scale covers ±480 pixels of the full-resolution image. Every step up, the offset is doubled and re-searched only within ±2 pixels, because the coarse answer is right up to the rounding of the halving. The strip above is the actual walk for the Emir's blue plate: six searches, 961 shifts on the tiny image and 25 on each of the others, less than a second in total for both plates, against tens of seconds for the SSIM runs that do the same walk.

Every parameter (the 10% scoring margin, the ±15 coarse window, the ±2 refinement, the 64-pixel floor) is the same for every image on this page. Nothing is tuned per plate.

d[i,j] = ¼ Σ s[2i+u, 2j+v]

for u, v ∈ {0, 1}: the box-filtered half-size image. At level k one pixel is 2k pixels of the scan, so an offset found there is multiplied by 2 on the way to level k − 1 and refined within ±2.

The pattern is clear. On raw pixels, L2 loses the church (red plate off by 271 pixels) and misses the Emir by 17, and NCC loses the Emir outright (red plate off by 582). All three fail for the same reason: the metric is comparing brightness, and a red filter and a blue filter see a scene completely differently. The Emir's robe is bright in the blue plate and nearly black in the red one, so the best "match" of brightness is nowhere near the true alignment. On gradient magnitudes, L2, NCC and SSIM agree on every plate, because edges sit in the same place no matter which filter produced them. LPIPS gets the Emir right even on raw pixels, since it is not really looking at brightness either; SSIM comes within 7 pixels of it.

SSIM, custom metric one. The structural similarity index compares two images through local statistics: for every 7×7 window it forms the two means, the two variances and the covariance, and combines them into a score in [−1, 1] that is high when the windows have the same local brightness, the same local contrast and the same structure. I compute all five maps with a 2-D integral image so each window sum is four array lookups, then average the map over the interior. I expected it to be the most robust score and it was the least: on raw pixels it never picks a wildly wrong offset, but on nine of the seventeen plates it settles 4 to 9 pixels away from where every other method agrees, and the fringes are visible (the church's steeple, the Ilemselga bell tower). The reason is the luminance term. SSIM rewards windows whose mean brightness matches, and between two filters mean brightness is exactly the thing that does not match, so the score is pulled toward shifts that pair bright patches with bright patches instead of edges with edges. On gradient magnitudes, where the brightness term becomes meaningless, SSIM agrees with NCC on every plate. It is also slow: every one of the 25 shifts at full resolution recomputes five 12-megapixel box filters.

LPIPS, custom metric two. The learned perceptual image patch similarity (Zhang et al., 2018) runs both images through a pretrained AlexNet, normalizes the activations of five layers, and takes a learned weighting of their squared differences. It was trained to agree with people about which of two distorted patches looks closer to a reference, which is a different question from alignment, but it means the score depends on features (edges, textures, parts of things) and not on which way the contrast goes across them. To keep it affordable I evaluate it on the central 512×512 of the overlap at every pyramid level, which is plenty of structure to pin a global translation, and run it on the GPU through PyTorch (the one library-heavy piece of this project, allowed for bells and whistles). On raw pixels it has no gross failure, the Emir included, and it runs at a speed between NCC and SSIM. It is not perfect: its score surface is noisier than NCC's, so its answers wander by a pixel or two on most plates and by 4 on the melons and the three generations, and on gradient images it loses the Ilemselga red plate by 21 pixels. That last one makes sense: AlexNet was trained on photographs, and an edge map is not a photograph. The other catch is that it is a black box, with no formula to point at for why it prefers a shift. It also needed its own pyramid settings: on a 100-pixel image the network's first stride-4 layer sees almost nothing, so its coarse search starts at 1/8 scale with a ±25 window instead of 1/32 with ±15, and the shifts are scored in batches of 32 on the GPU to keep that affordable. Both baselines are reported alongside these two, as the assignment asks.

SSIM = (2μaμb + c1)(2σab + c2) / (μa² + μb² + c1)(σa² + σb² + c2)

Computed per 7×7 window with c1 = 0.01², c2 = 0.03², then averaged. The five local moments come from one integral image each.

LPIPS = Σl ‖wl ⊙ (φ̂l(a) − φ̂l(b))‖² / HlWl

φ̂l are the unit-normalized activations of AlexNet layer l, wl the learned channel weights. The search maximizes −LPIPS.

1905→1915

Three plates of my own from the collection

I pulled three more master scans straight from the Library of Congress: a man on a camel in front of a mud-brick wall (the earliest, from Prokudin-Gorskii's Central Asian trip around 1905), the lakefront at Lugano from his travels in Switzerland the same year, and a study at the Kivach waterfall on the Suna river from 1915. I wanted one portrait with a subject that could move, one landscape with fine repeated structure, and one with water that is a different picture in every exposure.

All three aligned on the first try with the same parameters. The camel plate is fogged around its edges (the automatic crop takes a lot off it, see below), and its overall sepia cast is the one place where the white balance makes a large difference. The waterfall is the interesting one: the rushing water is a smooth blur in each plate and cannot help the alignment, so the score is carried entirely by the rocks, the trees and the man, which is exactly what you want and what the gradient features encourage.

542→40px

What went wrong, and why

With the basic pipeline (pyramid, NCC, raw pixel values) one of the fourteen plates fails: the Emir of Bukhara. The green plate lands correctly at (49, 24) but the red plate is placed at (300, −542), which is not a near miss, it is a different part of the search space entirely. The coarsest level is where it goes wrong: at 1/32 scale the blue plate shows a bright robe on a mid-gray wall and the red plate shows a dark robe on a bright wall, so the correlation of intensities is actually negative at the true offset, and the best correlation the search can find is a spurious match of the plate's edge against the door frame. Once the coarse level is wrong the refinements cannot recover, since they only look ±2 pixels around it.

The church fails the same way with L2 on raw pixels (red at (64, 267)) and for the same reason: L2 rewards the shift that makes the brightness histograms overlap, and a bright sky in the blue plate against a dark sky in the red one pulls it off the true answer. Both are fixed by scoring gradient magnitudes instead of pixel values, which is the "better features" item below and what every final image on this page uses. The one remaining blemish is not an alignment error: in the Ilemselga plate the red exposure fades toward the top edge, so the top of the sky comes out purple, and the automatic crop treats that band as border and removes it.

0→25%

Bells & whistles 1 · Automatic cropping

After alignment each composite is wrapped in junk: the scanner's paper-white margin, the black edge of the glass, the film rebate, and, along two sides of each moved plate, a strip where only one or two channels have any picture. The assignment is to find these edges rather than trim a fixed amount. I do it with a one-dimensional profile per side.

For every row I compute the fraction of its pixels that are border-like. A pixel is border-like if it is a gray extreme (near black or near white in all three channels: the glass edge and the paper) or strongly miscolored (two channels differ by more than half the range, which is what the rebate and the unshared strips look like once the plates have been shifted). A row is a border row if more than a third of it is border-like, or if nearly the whole row is paper white. Then, from each edge, I look inward through the outer quarter of the image for the innermost sustained run of border rows (at least 0.3% of the height) and cut just past it, plus a half-percent pad for the soft, vignetted edge of the rebate. The quarter only bounds the search: where the cut falls is measured. Isolated bad rows inside the picture, a saturated stripe of the Emir's robe for example, do not form a run and are ignored. Columns are handled identically.

The first version of this used a threshold relative to the interior's spread, and cut half of every image with a smooth sky, because a calm sky has almost no spread and anything else looks like an outlier next to it. The fixed, physical criteria above (what paper, glass and misregistration actually look like) turned out far more robust: the same numbers work on all seventeen plates and on the tiny JPEGs. The table lists what was removed. The camel plate loses the most because its edges are genuinely fogged; Ilemselga's top band is the one debatable call, discussed above.

0.5→99.5%ile

Bells & whistles 2 · Automatic contrast

The scans do not use the full range: the icon, for example, has its 0.5th and 99.5th percentiles at 0.08 and 0.93 once the borders are gone, and the wharf at 0.05 and 0.91. The safe fix is the one the assignment suggests. I find the 0.5th and 99.5th percentiles over all three channels together and map them linearly to 0 and 1, clipping the handful of pixels outside. Using percentiles instead of the true minimum and maximum means one speck of dust or one blown highlight cannot pin the range, and using the same two numbers for all three channels means the stretch changes brightness and contrast but not hue, so it cannot introduce a color cast (a per-channel stretch would, and that is what white balance is for).

For the nonlinear version I histogram-equalize the luminance, then blend only halfway from the original tone curve to the equalized one, and scale the three channels by the resulting luminance ratio so colors are left alone. Full equalization is too much for these photographs, it flattens the skies into gray and makes everything look like a poster, but half of it opens the shadows in the church interiors without wrecking the highlights. The final gallery uses the linear stretch plus a quarter-strength equalization, the same for every plate.

y = clip((x − p0.5) / (p99.5 − p0.5))

Applied identically to R, G and B. Then, optionally, L′ = (1 − s)L + s·cdf(L) on luminance with s = ½, and each channel multiplied by L′/L.

1→∞p

Bells & whistles 3 · Automatic white balance

White balance is two problems: guess the color of the light, then divide it out. The second half is easy and I do it the von Kries way, one gain per channel, normalized so green is untouched. The first half is the guess, and I implemented the three classic guesses, which turn out to be one formula with a knob. Gray world assumes the average color of the scene is gray, so the illuminant is the mean of each channel. White patch assumes the brightest thing in the scene is white, so the illuminant is the mean of the brightest 1% of pixels. Shades of gray (Finlayson and Trezzi) takes the Minkowski p-norm of each channel: p = 1 is gray world, p → ∞ is white patch, and p = 6 sits between them, weighting bright pixels more than the mean does but not staking everything on the single brightest patch.

Gray world overcorrects scenes that really are mostly one color (it turns the church's lake and sky beige, because it insists the picture average out to gray). White patch is at the mercy of whichever pixels happen to be brightest: on the camel plate that is the fogged edge, not a white object. Shades of gray with p = 6 is the one I use in the final gallery: it removes the blue cast the plates share, which I think is a property of the blue exposure being the reference, without flattening a genuinely blue scene. On the camel plate it takes out most of the sepia; the rest is real, that plate was photographed at midday in the desert.

ℓc = ( mean Icp )1/p

per channel c; then Ic ← Ic · ℓG / ℓc. With p = 1 this is gray world; as p → ∞ the norm tends to the maximum and it becomes white patch.

3×3

Bells & whistles 4 · A better color mapping, fitted to the Library's renditions

There is no reason the three filters Prokudin-Gorskii used in 1910 should correspond to the R, G and B primaries of a modern display. His "green" glass let through a broad band, his "red" was a deep orange-red, and the emulsion's sensitivity was anything but flat. Mapping the plates straight onto R, G and B is the zeroth-order guess, and it is why every direct composite has the same cyan-magenta look. A camera solves the same problem with a color correction matrix: a fixed 3×3 that mixes its sensor channels into the display's primaries. I wanted that matrix for these plates, and I wanted to fit it rather than eyeball it.

The reference I used is the Library of Congress itself. For every plate in the collection the Library publishes its own color rendition, made by hand from the same scan by its conservators. For four scenes (the Emir, and my three picks) I registered my composite to the Library's image and solved, by least squares over roughly half a million matching pixels, for the 3×3 matrix M that best predicts their colors from my plate values. Registration was the fiddly part: the Library's renditions are re-framed and cropped differently from a naive split into thirds, so I searched over a small grid of per-axis scales and then ran my own pyramid alignment on edge features to find the residual translation. The four matrices agree on their structure and I average them; the result is the single matrix on the right, applied unchanged to all seventeen plates, including the thirteen that had no say in the fit.

What the matrix says is physically sensible. The red row is nearly clean. The green row takes about 11% from each of the red and the blue plates, which is the broad green filter. The blue row is scaled down and borrows 11% from green, which desaturates the cyan cast. It does what a white balance does for the overall cast, but it also fixes hues that no per-channel gain can, like the pink-magenta shadows on skin and on white cloth. The remaining difference to the Library's versions is mostly tone and contrast, which their conservators set by hand per image and which no fixed matrix can copy.

Bells & whistles 5 · Better features: align the edges, not the brightness

The two failures in the metrics table share one cause: brightness is not a property of the scene, it is a property of the scene and the filter. A blue robe is bright through blue glass and black through red glass, so any score that compares brightness can prefer a wrong shift where bright happens to land on bright. Edges, on the other hand, are a property of the scene. The boundary between the robe and the wall is in the same place in every plate, whichever way the contrast goes across it.

So before scoring I replace each plate with its gradient magnitude, the length of the central-difference gradient at every pixel, computed by hand with two array subtractions. The pyramid is then built from the gradient images and the search is unchanged. With this one substitution all three metrics find the correct offset on all seventeen plates, and the offsets they find on the plates that already worked move by at most a pixel. I also tried a softer edge map (the gradient squashed through 1 − exp(−g/2ḡ), so that a few very strong edges do not dominate); it gives the same answers, so the plain magnitude is what the final pipeline uses.
