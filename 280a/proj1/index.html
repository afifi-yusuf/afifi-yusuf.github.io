−15→15px

Step 1 · Single-scale alignment

Reading the files. The files are JPG and TIF. I use imread to load each one as a NumPy array, convert it to float32, and scale it to [0, 1] (divide by 255 for the 8-bit JPGs, by 65535 for the 16-bit TIFs). If a scan comes in with 3 channels I average them into one, since the plate is grayscale anyway. The split function cuts the scan into the three stacked exposures using shape[0] divided by 3. The order on the plate is blue, green, red from top to bottom. 

def split(im):
    h = im.shape[0] // 3
    return im[:h], im[h:2*h], im[2*h:3*h]      # B, G, R

Comparing two plates. I keep the blue plate fixed and move green and red to match it. To say how well a shift matches I need a score. I first use the L2 norm, the Euclidean distance between the two plates (smaller is better, so I negate it so that every score reads "bigger is better"). Then I use NCC, normalized cross-correlation: subtract each plate's mean, divide by its length, and take the dot product. NCC does not care that a red filter and a blue filter let through different amounts of light, which L2 does. 

def l2(a, b):
    d = a - b
    return -np.sqrt(np.mean(d * d))

def ncc(a, b):
    a = a - a.mean(); b = b - b.mean()
    den = np.sqrt(np.sum(a * a) * np.sum(b * b))
    return np.sum(a * b) / den

The search. The align function takes the metric and the feature as parameters and does an exhaustive search: two for loops over every (dy, dx) from −15 to +15, 961 shifts, scoring each one and keeping the best. One detail matters a lot. The plates have black and white borders that are not part of the picture, and a shifted plate has an empty strip along two edges. So I only score the interior: I trim 10% from every side of the blue plate, and I take the matching pixels of the moving plate by slicing rather than with np.roll, so the strip that wraps around is never compared. Without this the search aligns the borders instead of the picture. 

for dy in range(-15, 16):
    for dx in range(-15, 16):
        m, r = crops(mov, ref, dy, dx, margin=0.1)   # interior slices
        grid[dy, dx] = metric(m, r)
best = argmax(grid)

What is from scratch. The assignment says to implement almost everything by hand. Everything in this section and the next is NumPy arithmetic: the split, the scores, the search, and later the downsampling and the pyramid. The only library calls in the alignment file read the image and write the JPEG. The bells and whistles further down are also plain NumPy, except two things the assignment allows for them: a resize when I fit the color matrix, and scipy's bilinear resampling in the two optional steps at the end.

where â = (a − mean a) / ‖a − mean a‖ and likewise for b. It is the cosine of the angle between the two plates as vectors.

1/32→1scale

Step 2 · Image pyramid

Why. The TIF plates are about 3700 × 3200 pixels and the offsets go up to 180 pixels. The step-1 search would need a window a few hundred pixels wide, tens of thousands of shifts, each one a pass over 12 million pixels. That is far too slow. So I search at a small scale first, where the offset is small, and refine.

Downsampling. I downsample with a 2×2 kernel and a stride of 2: every 2×2 block is averaged into one pixel, which halves the image. The averaging is the anti-aliasing filter. I do this repeatedly until the short side would drop under 64 pixels, which gives six levels for these scans: full size, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16 and 1/32. No library resize or pyramid function is used. 

def downsample(im):
    return 0.25 * (im[0::2, 0::2] + im[1::2, 0::2]
                   + im[0::2, 1::2] + im[1::2, 1::2])

Coarse to fine. I start at the 1/32 level, about 100 × 115 pixels, and run the same ±15 exhaustive search as step 1. At that scale ±15 covers ±480 pixels of the full image, more than any real offset. Then I move one level finer, double the offset (one pixel there is two pixels here), and search only ±2 around it, because the coarse answer is right up to rounding. I repeat until full resolution. The strip above is the real walk for the Emir's blue plate: (2, 1) at 1/32 becomes (49, 24) at full size. That is 961 shifts on a tiny image plus 25 on each of five larger ones, under a second for both plates. 

d = (0, 0)
for lvl in range(coarsest, -1, -1):

    d = align_single(pm[lvl], pr[lvl], radius, center=d)

All parameters are the same for every image: 10% scoring margin, ±15 coarse window, ±2 refinement, 64-pixel floor. Nothing is tuned per plate.

Where it fails: the Emir

Comparing raw pixels fails on the Emir. The green plate lands correctly at (49, 24), but the red plate is put at (300, −542), a completely different place. His robe averages 0.55 in the blue plate and 0.30 in the red plate. The wall behind him goes the other way: 0.49 in blue, 0.76 in red. So in the blue plate the robe is brighter than the wall, and in the red plate it is much darker than the wall. A score that compares brightness cannot line those up. At the true offset the raw NCC between the red and blue plates is only 0.264, and at the wrong offset the search found it is 0.307, so NCC genuinely preferred the wrong answer. It goes wrong at the coarsest level, and the ±2 refinements can never get back.

The church breaks the same way with L2 on raw pixels: red lands at (64, 267) because L2 rewards the shift where the bright sky in the blue plate overlaps something bright in the red plate. The common problem is that brightness depends on the filter, not just the scene. What does not depend on the filter is where the edges are.

pixels→edges

Step 3 · Better features: gradients bells & whistles 5

The gradient magnitude function makes an edge map of the image. It measures how much the brightness changes from one pixel to the next by taking the central difference in the vertical direction and in the horizontal direction, then combining the two with Pythagoras. Where the picture is flat the result is near zero; on an edge it is large. It is two array subtractions and a square root, no library. 

def gradient_magnitude(im):
    gy = np.zeros_like(im); gx = np.zeros_like(im)
    gy[1:-1] = (im[2:] - im[:-2]) * 0.5        # vertical change
    gx[:, 1:-1] = (im[:, 2:] - im[:, :-2]) * 0.5   # horizontal change
    return np.sqrt(gx * gx + gy * gy)

The point is that the robe's edge is in the same place in every plate, whichever way the contrast goes across it. So I run the gradient function on each plate first, build the pyramid from the gradient images, and search exactly as before. On the Emir the gradient NCC at the true offset is 0.493 and at the old wrong offset only 0.040, so the search has no reason to go anywhere else. The red plate lands at (107, 40). The church's L2 failure goes away too, and on the plates that were already fine the offsets move by at most one pixel. I also tried a softened edge map, 1 − exp(−g/2ḡ), so a few very strong edges do not dominate; it gave the same answers, so I kept the plain magnitude.

From here on, "aligned" means step 3: pyramid, NCC, gradients.

SSIM = (2μaμb + c1)(2σab + c2) / (μa² + μb² + c1)(σa² + σb² + c2)

Per 7×7 window with c1 = 0.01², c2 = 0.03², then averaged.

MI = H(a) + H(b) − H(a, b)

Entropies of the two 64-level histograms minus the entropy of their joint histogram, over the scored interior.

Every plate aligned eight ways: four scores on raw pixels, the same four on gradient magnitudes. Amber cells with a cross disagree with gradient NCC by more than 3 pixels, which is a visible fringe. The last column is the pyramid time for both plates. The first two column pairs are the basic metrics the assignment asks for. On raw pixels L2 loses the church (271 px off) and misses the Emir by 17; NCC loses the Emir (582 px). On gradients all four scores agree on every plate.

SSIM. Structural similarity compares two images through local statistics. For every 7×7 window it computes the two means, the two variances and the covariance, and combines them into a score that is high when the windows have the same local brightness, contrast and structure. I get all five maps from one integral image each, so every window sum is four lookups. I expected it to be the most robust score and it was the least: on raw pixels it settles 4 to 9 pixels off on nine of the seventeen plates, and you can see the fringes on the church's steeple. The reason is its luminance term, which rewards windows whose mean brightness matches, and between two filters that is exactly the thing that does not match. On gradients that term stops mattering and SSIM agrees with NCC everywhere. It is also slow, about 16 s per plate, because every shift at full resolution recomputes five 12-megapixel box filters. 

mu_a, mu_b = box(a), box(b)                      # 7x7 means via integral image
var_a, var_b = box(a*a) - mu_a**2, box(b*b) - mu_b**2
cov = box(a*b) - mu_a*mu_b
ssim = ((2*mu_a*mu_b + c1) * (2*cov + c2)) / ((mu_a**2 + mu_b**2 + c1) * (var_a + var_b + c2))

Mutual information. This metric fits the Emir problem exactly. Instead of asking whether the two plates are bright in the same places, it asks whether knowing a pixel's value in one plate tells you its value in the other, whichever way that relation goes. I quantize both plates to 64 levels, build their 64×64 joint histogram with one np.bincount, and compute H(a) + H(b) − H(a, b) from it. A robe that is bright in blue and black in red still gives a sharp joint histogram at the right shift, because "bright here" reliably means "dark there". Where it is better: on raw pixels it gets the Emir right, R (106, 40), which NCC and SSIM do not. Where it is weaker: with only 64 bins the score surface is a little flatter than NCC's, so it wanders by 1 to 4 pixels on the softer plates, and it is the one score that does not get sharper on gradients, since a gradient image is mostly near-zero and the histogram is dominated by that. It costs about 1.4 s per plate, twice NCC. 

ia, ib = (a * 64).astype(int), (b * 64).astype(int)          # quantize both plates
joint = np.bincount((ia * 64 + ib).ravel(), minlength=4096)   # 64x64 joint histogram
joint = joint / joint.sum()
pa, pb = joint.reshape(64, 64).sum(1), joint.reshape(64, 64).sum(0)
mi = H(pa) + H(pb) - H(joint)                                  # H(p) = -sum p log p

Step 5 · Automatic cropping bells & whistles 1

After alignment each image is wrapped in junk: the scanner's paper-white margin, the black edge of the glass, the film rebate, and, on two sides of each moved plate, a strip where only one or two channels have any picture. The assignment says to detect these edges rather than cut a fixed margin, so I do it with a one-dimensional profile per side.

The border profile. For each row, the border profile function asks three yes/no questions. Is nearly the whole row paper white (over 95% of pixels above 0.9 in all three channels)? Is more than a third of the row black in all three channels (below 0.08)? Is more than a third of the row strongly miscolored, meaning two channels differ by more than 0.5? The white catches the paper, the black catches the glass edge, and the miscolor catches the rebate and the strips where the plates do not overlap. Real picture content, even a bright sky or a saturated robe, rarely fills a whole row like that. Columns get the same three questions. 

white    = (rgb > 0.9).all(axis=2).mean(axis=1) > 0.95
black    = (rgb < 0.08).all(axis=2)
miscolor = max(|r-g|, |g-b|, |r-b|) > 0.5
bad_row  = white | ((black | miscolor).mean(axis=1) > 0.35)

The walk. Then I scan inward from each edge through the outer quarter of the image and look for the innermost sustained run of bad rows, at least 0.3% of the height in a row, and cut just past it, plus a half-percent pad for the soft edge of the rebate. The quarter only bounds the search; where the cut lands is measured. A single bad row inside the picture does not form a run, so it is ignored. 

cut, run = 0, 0
for i in range(limit):            # from the edge inward, outer quarter
    run = run + 1 if bad[i] else 0
    if run >= min_run:
        cut = i + 1               # innermost sustained run so far

Two passes. The strict pass above leaves thin colored fringes where a plate fades toward its edge, because that band is off by 0.3 rather than 0.5. So I run a second, gentler pass on the result (half the row off by 0.3), but only within the outer 6%, where real content cannot form a sustained run. My first version of all this used a threshold relative to how much the interior varied, and it cut half of every image with a calm sky, because a sky varies so little that everything else looked like an outlier. Describing what paper, glass and misregistration physically look like was far more robust.

When it works and when it does not. It works cleanly on the church, the three generations and the harvesters above: the frame and the rebate go, the picture stays, including the harvesters' overexposed sky. It is wrong in three places. On Ilemselga the red exposure fades toward the top edge, so the top of the sky comes out purple, and the detector treats that band as border and removes 35% of the plate. On the camel plate the edges really are fogged, and it takes 26%. On the lilacs the second pass takes a strip of real flowers off the left side, because that plate is soft and purple all the way to its edge, so the gentler miscolor test cannot tell the fringe from the picture there. All three come from the same criteria, not from tuning, and I left them as they are.

Step 6 · Better color mapping bells & whistles 4

[R G B]ᵀ = M · [r g b]ᵀ

0.94 +0.01 +0.06 +0.11 0.75 +0.11 -0.01 +0.11 0.85

Least squares over registered pixels of four scenes against the Library's prokc versions; mean of the four fits. Rows are output R, G, B; columns are the red, green and blue plates.

Why. There is no reason the three filters Prokudin-Gorskii used in 1910 match the R, G and B primaries of a modern screen. His green glass let through a broad band, his red was a deep orange-red, and the emulsion was not equally sensitive to everything. Mapping the plates straight onto R, G and B is the zeroth-order guess, and it is why every image so far has the same cyan-magenta look. A camera fixes the same problem with a color correction matrix, a fixed 3×3 that mixes its sensor channels into the display's primaries. I fit this matrix instead of guessing it.

How. The Library of Congress publishes its own color version of every plate, made by hand by its conservators from the same scan. For four scenes (the Emir and my three picks) I registered my image to theirs and solved, by least squares over about half a million matching pixels, for the 3×3 matrix M that best predicts their colors from my plate values. Registration was the hard part: their versions are framed and cropped differently from a split into equal thirds, so I searched a small grid of per-axis scales and then ran my own pyramid alignment on gradient features to find the residual shift. The four matrices agree in structure, so I average them, and that single matrix is applied to all seventeen plates, including the thirteen that had no say in the fit (three generations and melons above are two of those). 

M, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(mine, loc)     # loc ≈ mine @ M, over registered pixels
rgb_out = np.clip(rgb @ M.T, 0, 1)      # one matrix multiply per pixel

What it says. The red row is nearly identity. The green row takes about 11% from each of the red and blue plates, which is the broad green filter. The blue row is scaled down to 0.85 and borrows 11% from green, which desaturates the cyan cast. It does part of what the white balance does for the overall cast, but it also fixes hues no per-channel gain can, like the pink shadows on skin and white cloth. The difference that remains against the Library's versions is mostly tone and contrast, which their conservators set by hand per image and which no fixed matrix can copy. What is left on the church after this step is a blue cast that every plate shares, which is the next step.

1→6p

Step 7 · Automatic white balance bells & whistles 3

ℓc = ( mean Icp )1/p

per channel c; then Ic ← Ic · ℓG / ℓc. With p = 1 this is gray world; as p → ∞ the norm tends to the maximum and it becomes white patch.

White balance is two problems: guess the color of the light (the illuminant), then divide it out. The second half is easy. The illuminant function returns one RGB triple; the white balance function divides each channel by it, normalized so green stays put, which keeps the overall brightness about the same. The hard half is the guess, and I implemented the three classic guesses. Gray world assumes the average color of the scene is gray, so the illuminant is the mean of each channel. White patch assumes the brightest thing in the scene is white, so the illuminant is the mean of the brightest 1% of pixels. Shades of gray takes the Minkowski p-norm of each channel: p = 1 is gray world, p → ∞ is white patch, and p = 6 sits in between, weighting bright pixels more than the mean does without betting everything on the single brightest patch. 

def illuminant(rgb, p=6):
    return np.mean(rgb.reshape(-1, 3) ** p, axis=0) ** (1 / p)   # p=1 gray world, p→∞ white patch

def white_balance(rgb):
    ill = illuminant(rgb)
    return np.clip(rgb * (ill[1] / ill), 0, 1)                   # green untouched

What I saw. Gray world overcorrects scenes that really are mostly one color: it turns the church's lake and sky beige because it insists the whole picture average out to gray. White patch depends on whichever pixels happen to be brightest, and on the camel plate that is the fogged edge, not a white object. Shades of gray with p = 6 is what I kept. It removes the blue cast the plates share, which I think comes from the blue exposure being the reference, without flattening a scene that really is blue. On the camel it takes out most of the sepia; the rest is real, it was shot at midday in the desert.

0.5→99.5%ile

Step 8 · Automatic contrast bells & whistles 2

y = clip((x − p0.5) / (p99.5 − p0.5))

Applied identically to R, G and B. Then L′ = (1 − s)L + s·cdf(L) on luminance, and each channel multiplied by L′/L.

The stretch. The scans do not use the full range. The icon, for example, has its 0.5th and 99.5th percentiles at 0.08 and 0.93 once the borders are gone, and the wharf at 0.05 and 0.91. I find those two percentiles over all three channels together and map them linearly to 0 and 1, clipping the few pixels outside. Percentiles instead of the true min and max means one speck of dust or one blown highlight cannot pin the range. Using the same two numbers for all three channels changes brightness and contrast but not hue, so this step cannot add a color cast, which matters because it runs after the white balance. 

lo, hi = np.percentile(rgb, 0.5), np.percentile(rgb, 99.5)
rgb = np.clip((rgb - lo) / (hi - lo), 0, 1)

Histogram equalization. For the nonlinear part I equalize the luminance: build its histogram, take the cumulative sum, normalize it, and that CDF is the new tone curve. Then I only blend part of the way from the original curve to the equalized one, and scale all three channels by the same luminance ratio so colors stay put. Full equalization is too much for these photos, it flattens skies into gray and makes everything look like a poster. Half strength, shown above, opens the shadows in the church interiors without wrecking the highlights. The final pipeline uses a quarter strength on every plate. With this the church is finished; compare it with the stacked plates back in step 2. 

hist, edges = np.histogram(y, bins=1024, range=(0, 1))
cdf = np.cumsum(hist) / hist.sum()
y_eq = np.interp(y, edges[:-1], cdf)
y_new = (1 - s) * y + s * y_eq                 # s = 0.25 in the final pipeline
rgb = rgb * (y_new / y)[..., None]

0.1°

Step 9 · Local alignment: rotation, scale and motion optional · better transformations

[y′ x′] = s·R(θ)·[y x] + t

Fitted by least squares to the trusted tile shifts, around the image centre. Every red plate comes out with a small negative θ, and on 13 of the 14 plates the red is rotated more than the green.

Why. Two things cannot be fixed by sliding a whole plate: the camel rider moved between the three exposures, and the plates may differ by a small rotation or scale, not just a shift. A brute-force search over rotation and scale would add two dimensions to every level of the pyramid, so instead I measure them. After the global alignment I cut the moving plate into 320-pixel tiles and re-align each tile on its own, with the same gradient NCC search, over ±10 pixels. That gives one arrow per tile: how far that part of the plate still wants to move. 

for each 320x320 tile (ty, tx), skipping the outer 8%:
    r = grad_ref[ty:ty+320, tx:tx+320]
    best over dy, dx in [-10, 10]: ncc(grad_mov[ty-dy:ty-dy+320, tx-dx:tx-dx+320], r)
    trust the tile if peak ncc > 0.35 and the tile has texture

Trusting a tile. A tile of blank sky or flat sand has no edges to lock onto, so its arrow is noise. I only keep tiles whose gradient NCC peak is above 0.35 and whose gradient image has some energy; the gray arrows in the figure are the ones thrown away. On the camel plate 106 of 144 tiles are trusted; on the church, which is mostly sky and lake, only 12, and the fit there rests on those.

Rotation and scale for free. A least-squares fit of a similarity transform, rotation θ, scale s and a translation, through the trusted arrows gives the two extra dimensions the assignment suggests searching for, without searching. The result is systematic: on every one of the 14 large plates the red exposure comes out rotated by −0.03° to −0.29°, the green by less, in the same direction on all but Lugano's green (+0.04°), and on 13 of the 14 the red plate is rotated more than the green. Red was exposed last, so the plate holder crept the same way each time. The scales are within 0.4% of one. A tenth of a degree is 5 pixels at the corner of a 3700-pixel plate, which is exactly the size of fringe the global alignment leaves at the edges. 

A = [[y, -x, 1, 0], [x, y, 0, 1]] per trusted tile      # unknowns a = s cos θ, b = s sin θ, ty, tx
(a, b, ty, tx) = lstsq(A, tile_centre + tile_shift)
theta = atan2(b, a); scale = hypot(a, b)

The local warp. For the rider I need more than a rigid transform, so I turn the arrows into a dense field: untrusted tiles borrow the nearest trusted arrow, the coarse grid is smoothed with a small Gaussian, and it is upsampled to every pixel. Each plate is then resampled so pixel (y, x) takes its value from (y − dy, x − dx). That is a bilinear lookup through scipy.ndimage.map_coordinates, a library call the assignment allows for bells and whistles. The field follows the rider because his tiles disagree with the tiles around him, and the smoothing keeps the seams between tiles from showing.

What it does and does not fix. Gradient NCC between the plates goes up on every plate, most on the icon (0.35 → 0.57) and the wharf (0.58 → 0.79), where the tenth-of-a-degree rotation was leaving fringes along the edges. The rider's face and hat come back into register, and at the corners of the icon and the self-portrait, where a tenth of a degree is five pixels, the chandelier and the flowers lose their fringes. Kivach's water does not improve, and cannot: the water is a different picture in each exposure, not a shifted one, so there is no arrow that describes it. I keep this as a separate step rather than part of the final gallery, because a warp can invent detail where the arrows are wrong, and I would rather the seventeen finished plates be the plain, honest translation.

±20px

Step 10 · Stereographs: depth from the same alignment extra · external data

Z ∝ 1 / d

Depth is inversely proportional to disparity d, the horizontal shift of a point between the two frames. The map shows d itself, stretched between its 2nd and 98th percentiles, so it is ordinal depth, not metres.

Why. A Prokudin-Gorskii plate is three exposures of one scene that differ by a filter. A stereograph card is two exposures of one scene that differ by a few centimetres of viewpoint. The registration problem is identical, and the Library of Congress has thousands of these cards from the same years and places, so I pulled three: Nevsky Prospect in St. Petersburg in 1901 and 1906, and the Kremlin from across the river in 1905. The difference is what the leftover shift means. On a plate, any shift that survives the global alignment is an error or a subject that moved. On a stereograph it is depth.

Finding the frames. The scan is a whole card, black background and all. The card is the bright box; the two photographs sit symmetrically about its centre, so on each half I take the longest run of columns, then rows, whose intensity spread is high. The printed margins have spread too, but they are short runs. A blank sky can end a run early, so the two boxes are equalized to the larger one, each anchored at its outer edge.

Registration. Step 2's pyramid, with step 3's gradient features, aligns the right frame to the left one; the offsets are real (213, −48) on the 1901 card, since the two prints were mounted by hand. Then step 9's tile search runs again, but only along the horizontal axis (±30 px, with ±2 px vertical slack), because that is the direction the two lenses were separated in. Each trusted tile's horizontal shift is its disparity. Untrusted tiles, mostly sky, borrow their nearest trusted neighbour, and the coarse grid is smoothed and upsampled as in step 9. The bounding box of the trusted tiles is also what crops away the card, since the margin text differs between the two halves and never gets trusted. 

d = align_pyramid(right, left, metric="ncc", feature="grad")      # global: (213, -48) on the 1901 card
right = shift(right, *d)
for each 100x100 tile: best dx in [-30, 30], dy in [-2, 2] by gradient NCC   # disparity
field = dense_field(centres, shifts, keep)                          # smooth, fill from nearest trusted tile
depth = colormap(field[1])                                          # horizontal shift = disparity ∝ 1/Z
anaglyph = stack(left, right, right)                                # red = left, cyan = right

What it finds. On the 1901 card the bridge statue, the lamp post and the people on the pavement come out amber and the far end of the Prospect indigo, and the disparity runs over about 22 pixels between the nearest and farthest trusted tiles. The 1906 card does the same with the carriages. The anaglyph puts the left frame in red and the registered right frame in cyan, which is the stereo pair's own 3D if you have the glasses; the wiggle above is the version that needs none. The Kremlin card is the failure case: the whole scene is across a river, over a hundred metres away, so its disparity is ±3 pixels, below what 100-pixel tiles can measure, and the map is noise. Depth from a stereo pair needs something near the camera.
