Stitching photo mosaics

When the camera only turns, every pair of views is related by one 3×3 homography. I solve for it from points I clicked, warp each photo through it, and blend the warped photos into one picture. Hraunfossar, 142° wide, goes onto a cylinder.

Part A.2 · Recover the homographies

w[x′ y′ 1]ᵀ = H[x y 1]ᵀ

With h33 = 1, multiplying out the divide gives two linear equations per point:

xh11 + yh12 + h13 − xx′h31 − yx′h32 = x′

xh21 + yh22 + h23 − xy′h31 − yy′h32 = y′

Least squares. H has 8 unknowns, so 4 points determine it exactly and leave nothing to absorb a misplaced click. I clicked 6–10 points per pair in a small browser tool and solve the 2n×8 system Ah = b with np.linalg.lstsq. Before solving, both point sets are moved to their centroid and scaled to a mean distance of √2 (Hartley normalization). Otherwise the columns of A range from 1 to 10⁶. Here the normalized and raw solves map the clicks to within 0.1 px of each other.

def computeH(im1_pts, im2_pts, normalize=True):
    p = np.asarray(im1_pts, np.float64)
    q = np.asarray(im2_pts, np.float64)
    T1 = _normalizer(p) if normalize else np.eye(3)
    T2 = _normalizer(q) if normalize else np.eye(3)
    A, b = homography_system(apply_h(T1, p), apply_h(T2, q))
    h = np.linalg.lstsq(A, b, rcond=None)[0]
    H = np.linalg.inv(T2) @ np.append(h, 1).reshape(3, 3) @ T1
    return H / H[2, 2]

Where I clicked. I chose sharp, static points spread across each overlap: truss joints, the corners of the scoreboard cube, pitch markings, rock outcrops and shoreline corners. I avoided water, clouds, people and screen content, because they change between frames.

Part A.3 · Warp the images and rectify

Inverse warping. For every pixel of the output I apply H−1 to find where it comes from in the source, and sample the source there. Sampling this way leaves no holes, which forward-splatting source pixels would. The output box is the warped image's four corners, rounded out to whole pixels. The alpha mask is true wherever the source point lands inside the photo.

    ...                                        # the closest pixel centre

def sample_bilinear(im, xs, ys):
    x0, y0 = np.floor(xs).astype(int), np.floor(ys).astype(int)
    fx, fy = xs - x0, ys - y0
    top = (1 - fx) * im[y0, x0] + fx * im[y0, x0 + 1]
    bot = (1 - fx) * im[y0 + 1, x0] + fx * im[y0 + 1, x0 + 1]
    return (1 - fy) * top + fy * bot           # the four closest, by distance

Both samplers match OpenCV's warpPerspective, which I use only as a check, on all but 0.01% of interior pixels. Pixel (0, 0) is the centre of the top-left pixel, so a pixel covers ±0.5 around its integer coordinates.

Rectification. This uses the same computeH, but only one side of each correspondence is clicked. The other side is where that point would be in a straight-on view: the corners of a rectangle with the object's measured proportions. Because the object is flat, one H maps it correctly however the camera was placed. Anything off the plane, like the wall and the depth of the frame, comes out sheared.

Speed against quality. Bilinear is about 3 times slower: it reads four pixels and does three interpolations where nearest neighbour reads one. The difference only shows when the warp enlarges the photo. The close-ups above are the same regions rectified 6 times larger, straight from the photo. Nearest neighbour turns each source pixel into a slanted block with stair-stepped edges, and bilinear blends between them. At the sizes above, where the warp mostly shrinks, the two are hard to tell apart. Everything else on this page uses bilinear, since a mosaic takes 1–4 s either way.

1→3blends

Part A.4 · Blend the images into a mosaic

Procedure.

  1. The middle frame is the reference. Each neighbour's H comes from its clicks; frames further out compose the pairs (H2→3 for frame 2, H4→3 = H3→4−1 for frame 4).
  2. The canvas is the bounding box of every frame's warped corners.
  3. Each frame is inverse-warped onto the canvas once, with bilinear sampling. A fourth channel goes along with it: each pixel's distance to the nearest edge of its own frame, which is 0 outside the frame.
  4. The warped frames are blended, using that distance as the weight.

Three blends. Overwrite pastes each frame over the last, which gives hard seams wherever exposure differs. Feather is the weighted average, so seams fade, but anything that moved between frames shows twice. Two-band splits each frame at a Gaussian of σ = 8 px. The low band is feathered, which evens out exposure across the overlap. The high band at each pixel comes from the frame with the largest weight there, so detail is never averaged into a ghost. That choice is softened over 6 px, so drifting clouds don't meet in a hard edge.

AT&T Stadium · 3 frames, 19 points

To line up the roof I had to click it. With all my points below it, the roof came out about 20 px off, because the fit was extrapolating up there. Two points on the trusses fixed it.

Hetch Hetchy · 3 frames, 14 points

One hiker. The hiker walked between frames, so each overlap holds him once over background in the other frame, and no weighting can pick one copy. I compare each frame with the reference after a 2 px blur. Wherever they differ by more than 0.12, I fade the non-reference frame's weight to zero over 12 px. That leaves the hiker as frame 3 saw him. The same test catches the near granite, which is 5–30 px off between frames: it is a few metres away, and a hand-held phone does not turn exactly about its lens. The ridge and shoreline, where I clicked, agree to within 1 px and are left alone.

6.2°→0°tilt

Part A.5 · Cylindrical projection Bells & whistles · 280A

θ = u / f

x = f tan θ, y = v / cos θ

A canvas pixel (u, v) on the unrolled cylinder is a ray at angle θ from the reference view. It hits the reference image plane at (x, y), and Hi−1 takes that point into frame i.

Sampled once, from the originals. Each canvas pixel follows the map on the right, through Hi−1, into each original frame, and is sampled there with the same bilinear sampler and blended with the same two-band blend. Nothing is resampled twice, so the cylinder is as sharp as the flat mosaic.

Focal length from the homographies. If the camera only rotates, Hi = KRiK−1 with K the camera matrix. I search for the f that makes K−1HiK closest to a rotation for the three other frames at once. That gives f = 1225 px, or 22.1 mm in 35 mm terms; the iPhone 15 Pro's main camera is rated at 24 mm. With it, the frames span 81.6° of panning and 142° of view.

Levelling. I held the phone at a slight angle while panning, so the axis I turned about leans 6.2° from the reference camera's vertical. A cylinder around that vertical bends the horizon into an arc, so I recover each frame's rotation from Hi = KRiK−1, take the pan axis from the first frame to the last, and rotate the reference view so the axis points straight up. That is Hi ← KQK−1Hi, where Q is the smallest rotation that does it. A synthetic check with 8° of tilt recovers 8.00°.

On land the seams agree to within about 1 px, and to within 5 px near the horizon at the 3 → 4 seam. The clouds drifted during the 1.5 s between frames, and the 6 px soft seam in the high band hides that.
