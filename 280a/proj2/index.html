Part 1.1 · Convolution from scratch

Convolution. A convolution is a sliding weighted sum. A kernel smaller than the image slides over it, and each output pixel is the dot product of the flipped kernel with the window under it. With weights that sum to 1, like the box filter, it is a moving average. Without padding, an n×n image and a k×k filter give an (n−k+1)×(n−k+1) output, so I pad the image with zeros to keep the resolution: k//2 before and (k−1)//2 after for same, k−1 on every side for full.

def pad_zero(im, kh, kw, mode):
    if mode == "full":
        top, bottom, left, right = kh - 1, kh - 1, kw - 1, kw - 1
    elif mode == "same":
        top, bottom, left, right = kh // 2, (kh - 1) // 2, kw // 2, (kw - 1) // 2
    H, W = im.shape
    out = np.zeros((H + top + bottom, W + left + right))
    out[top:top + H, left:left + W] = im
    return out

Four loops. Two over the output pixels, two over the kernel taps.

def conv2d_four(im, kernel, mode="same"):
    kh, kw = kernel.shape
    k = kernel[::-1, ::-1]                      # convolution flips the filter
    p = pad_zero(im, kh, kw, mode)
    H, W = p.shape[0] - kh + 1, p.shape[1] - kw + 1
    out = np.zeros((H, W))
    for i in range(H):
        for j in range(W):
            acc = 0.0
            for u in range(kh):
                for v in range(kw):
                    acc += k[u, v] * p[i + u, j + v]
            out[i, j] = acc
    return out

Two loops. The inner two loops become one NumPy operation: kernel times window, summed.

    for i in range(H):
        for j in range(W):
            out[i, j] = np.sum(k * p[i:i + kh, j:j + kw])

Runtime. Two loops is 8 times faster than four for the 81-tap box, and slower for the 2-tap derivative filters, where the fixed cost of a NumPy call is more than two multiplications. scipy is 20 to 200 times faster than both.

Boundaries. Zero padding darkens the border. After the box filter the top row averages 0.19 against 0.34 five rows in: a dark frame 4 pixels wide. scipy with zero fill does the same. For derivatives the frame becomes an edge of its own, so from part 1.2 on I mirror the border.

Derivatives are drawn as 0.5 + 2 × value, so gray is zero. Dx responds to vertical edges (the pole, the sides of the glasses), Dy to horizontal ones (the brow, the moustache).

0.10→0.30threshold

Part 1.2 · Finite difference operator

Partial derivatives. Partial derivatives come from the high-pass kernels Dx = [1 −1] and its transpose Dy: each output is the difference between a pixel and its neighbour. The gradient magnitude is √(gx² + gy²), the strength of the change in any direction, and an edge is where it is large.

gx, gy = conv(im, DX), conv(im, DY)
mag = np.sqrt(gx * gx + gy * gy)
edges = mag > threshold

Input. The course PNG has a white margin (7 rows on top, 1 below, 1 column left, 5 right). It came out as the strongest edge in the picture, so I crop it, and I mirror the border instead of zero filling for the same reason.

Threshold. I chose 0.20. At 0.10 and 0.15 the grass is speckle: a two-pixel difference cannot tell texture from an edge. At 0.25 and 0.30 the buildings and the far tripod leg break up. 0.20 keeps the man, the camera, the tripod and the skyline, at the cost of a little speckle.

2→1convolutions

Part 1.3 · Derivative of Gaussian filter

Blur first. A Gaussian is a low-pass filter: it averages neighbouring pixels, which blurs. The speckle is high-frequency and a difference of neighbours amplifies it, so I blur before differentiating. The 1D kernel is from cv2.getGaussianKernel, the outer product makes it 2D, σ = 2, width 6σ + 1 = 13.

g = cv2.getGaussianKernel(13, 2.0)     # 13 x 1
G = g @ g.T                            # 13 x 13
blurred = conv(im, G)
gx, gy = conv(blurred, DX), conv(blurred, DY)

What is different. No grass speckle at any threshold. Edges are thick and continuous, because a blurred edge is a ramp several pixels wide. The cost is detail: the small parts of the camera merge. Magnitudes drop from 0.87 to 0.16, since each step is spread over more pixels, so the threshold drops too. 0.05 keeps the buildings; 0.07 loses them.

One convolution. Convolution is associative, (image ∗ G) ∗ Dx = image ∗ (G ∗ Dx), so I convolve G with Dx and Dy once and get derivative of Gaussian filters that blur and differentiate in one pass.

dog_x = convolve2d(G, DX, mode="full")     # 13 x 14
dog_y = convolve2d(G, DY, mode="full")     # 14 x 13
gx, gy = conv(im, dog_x), conv(im, dog_y)

Same result. Each DoG filter is a bump next to a dip. One pass and two passes differ by at most 1e-15, and the edge maps agree on every pixel at all five thresholds.

0→360degrees

Gradient orientationBells & whistles · part 1

HSV. The gradient direction goes in the hue, which is a cyclic colormap just as angles are cyclic, and the magnitude goes in the value, so flat regions stay dark. Saturation is 1.

The angle without an angle function. No arctan2 or np.angle. I use CORDIC: fold the vector into the right half plane, then rotate it onto the x axis in 24 steps of atan(2−i), each one clockwise or anticlockwise, whichever shrinks y. The signed sum of the steps is the angle. The step angles come from the arctangent power series, so no library angle function is used anywhere.

CORDIC_ANGLES = [pi / 4] + [atan_series(2.0 ** -i) for i in range(1, 24)]

def orientation(gx, gy):
    x, y = gx.copy(), gy.copy()
    left = x < 0                                # fold into the right half plane
    x[left], y[left] = -x[left], -y[left]
    theta = np.zeros_like(x)
    for i, a in enumerate(CORDIC_ANGLES):
        d = np.where(y >= 0, 1.0, -1.0)         # which way shrinks y
        x, y = x + d * y * 2.0 ** -i, y - d * x * 2.0 ** -i
        theta += d * a
    theta[left] += pi
    return np.mod(theta, 2 * pi)

Checked against np.arctan2: the largest difference is 1.2e-07 radians. HSV to RGB is also written by hand and matches colorsys to 1e-16.

Reading it. Red points right (dark to bright), yellow-green up, cyan left, purple down. The coat's left edge is cyan because the bright sky is to its left. Each tripod leg shows as two lines in opposite colours, one per side. At full brightness (second picture) flat regions show the direction of noise, which is why the magnitude goes in the value.

0.5→4α

Part 2.1 · Image sharpening

Unsharp mask. To sharpen an image I blur it and subtract the blur from the original, which leaves the high frequencies: edges and fine texture. Adding them back scaled by α amplifies them, and the image looks sharper.

One filter. sharpened = image + α (image − image ∗ G) = image ∗ ((1 + α) δ − α G), where δ is the unit impulse. The weights sum to 1, so flat regions keep their brightness.

def unsharp_kernel(sigma, alpha):
    g = cv2.getGaussianKernel(2 * ceil(3 * sigma) + 1, sigma)
    k = -alpha * (g @ g.T)
    k[k.shape[0] // 2, k.shape[1] // 2] += 1 + alpha
    return k

sharpened = convolve2d(channel, unsharp_kernel(sigma, alpha), mode="same", boundary="symm")

One filter and three steps agree to 1e-15. Colour images are filtered per channel.

Varying α. At 0.5 and 1 the Taj Mahal just looks better focused. At 2 the dome gets a dark outline, and at 4 a halo, with the JPEG blocks in the sky showing. The halo is the negative ring of the filter. The captions count the values clipped outside [0, 1]: detail destroyed, not added.

My own picture. A wall I photographed for Project 0: brushed concrete, lettering, palm fronds. It is 1000 pixels tall, so σ = 2.

A blurry picture. My zoomed selfie from Project 0, soft from digital zoom. At α = 2 the curls separate and the glasses get their edges back, with no halos, because a soft picture has little high-frequency content to overshoot with.

Evaluation: blur, then sharpen. I blurred the sharp wall with σ = 2 and sharpened it back at the same σ. The lettering recovers: its edges are steep again. The concrete does not: the brush strokes are gone and stay gone.

High-frequency energy (RMS of picture − its blur) is 0.047 in the original, 0.010 blurred, and back to 0.045 at α = 8. But the RMS error against the original only improves from 0.047 to 0.040 (at α = 2), and at α = 8 it is 0.061, worse than doing nothing. The blur multiplied the finest frequencies by nearly zero. The unsharp mask multiplies by at most 1 + α, and that times nearly zero is still nearly zero. It steepens surviving edges. It cannot bring back what was removed.

9→14σ px

Part 2.2 · Hybrid images

The idea. A hybrid image adds the high-pass of one image to the low-pass of another. Up close the fine detail dominates. From far away it cannot be resolved and only the low frequencies are left, so the picture changes with distance.

Alignment. The two pictures must agree where it matters, or the eye sees two overlapping things. Instead of clicking two points per picture I store them in a file (the eyes) and compute the similarity transform that carries the cat's eyes onto Derek's.

t = SimilarityTransform.from_estimate(cat_eyes, derek_eyes)
cat_aligned = warp(cat, t.inverse, output_shape=derek.shape[:2], mode="edge", order=3)

The filters. Low-pass is a Gaussian. High-pass is the impulse minus a Gaussian: the picture minus its blur.

def hybrid(low_im, high_im, s_low, s_high):
    low = blur(low_im, s_low)
    high = high_im - blur(high_im, s_high)      # impulse minus Gaussian
    return np.clip(low + high, 0, 1)

Choosing the cutoffs. A Gaussian of width σ passes half the amplitude at f = √(ln 2 / 2) / (πσ) cycles per pixel. In the grid the low-pass σ goes down and the high-pass σ goes across. Low-pass too narrow (σ = 4): Derek keeps his eyes and teeth and competes with the cat up close. Too wide (16): he is a blob from far away. High-pass too narrow (6): the cat is only whiskers. Too wide (24): the cat is still visible from far away. I chose σ = 9 for Derek (0.021 cycles per pixel) and σ = 14 for the cat (0.013). The bands overlap slightly, which is better than a gap where neither picture reads.

In the frequency domain. log |FFT|, zero frequency in the centre. Derek's spectrum falls off quickly: a smooth face on a plain background. The cat's is broader, from the fur, with diagonal streaks from the shelf and the wall. The low-pass keeps a small disc in the centre, the high-pass everything but the centre, and the hybrid is one inside the other. The thin cross along the axes is the border of the picture: the FFT treats the picture as repeating, and the jump from one side to the other is an edge that no blur inside the picture removes.

Where colour helps. In the low frequencies. Splitting each picture into gray and chroma, the low-pass keeps 93% of Derek's chroma energy and the high-pass keeps 4% of the cat's: colour changes slowly, over regions, and a high-pass removes regions. Tiger and cat. The same face plan, so once the eyes are aligned everything else nearly is. Tiger low (σ = 7), cat high (σ = 9): a hissing tabby up close, a tiger at one eighth size. The other way round fails, because a tiger's stripes are broad and so are low-frequency themselves. The cat's ears survive at a distance: an outline against a dark background is a strong edge at every scale. This is my favourite, so its spectra are below. Both are nearly the same in every direction, since fur has no preferred direction. The low-pass leaves a small disc, the high-pass a dark hole in the centre.

Shades: my own photos. Two photos of me from a propped phone, one in mirrored glasses. The eyes are behind the lens, so there are no two points to click. Part 2.3 · Gaussian and Laplacian stacks

Stacks. A pyramid blurs and halves the picture at each level; a stack blurs and keeps the size. Level 0 of the Gaussian stack is the picture, and each level is the previous one blurred again, with σ = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, for 6 levels. Doubling σ stands in for the halving. No pyramid functions: the only library call is the Gaussian blur.

Laplacian stack. Each level is the difference of two neighbouring Gaussian levels, the band of frequencies the second blur removed, and the last level is the last Gaussian level. The differences telescope, so the stack sums back to the picture (to 1e-16). That is what makes blending possible: change each band separately, then add them up.

def gaussian_stack(im, levels, sigma0=1.0):
    out = [im]
    for k in range(1, levels):
        out.append(blur(out[-1], sigma0 * 2 ** (k - 1)))
    return out

def laplacian_stack(im, levels, sigma0=1.0):
    g = gaussian_stack(im, levels, sigma0)
    return [g[k] - g[k + 1] for k in range(levels - 1)] + [g[-1]]

Laplacian levels are drawn with gray as zero, each stretched to its own range. The last level is a Gaussian level, drawn as it is.

Part 2.4 · Multiresolution blending

The blend. A straight cut shows a seam because the join is abrupt at every frequency. A narrow feather leaves the colour as a step; a wide one ghosts the textures. Burt and Adelson's answer is a width per frequency: I blend each band of the Laplacian stack with its own mask width, fine detail over a few pixels and broad colour over about a quarter of the picture.

I follow the algorithm on page 230 of the paper. The mask is a step, 1 on the apple's side. Its Gaussian stack uses the same σ as the pictures, so level k of the mask is blurred to the scale of band k. Each band is mask × apple + (1 − mask) × orange, and the result is the sum of the bands.

def blend(a, b, mask, levels=6):
    la, lb = laplacian_stack(a, levels), laplacian_stack(b, levels)
    gm = gaussian_stack(mask, levels)
    bands = [gm[k] * la[k] + (1 - gm[k]) * lb[k] for k in range(levels)]
    return np.clip(sum(bands), 0, 1)

The Gaussian stack of the mask. Mk weights band k: the finest band is joined along a hard line and the coarsest over a wide ramp.

The figure. Figure 3.42 of Szeliski for my stacks: bands 0, 2 and 4, then the sum. In band 0 the halves meet along a sharp line, which is right, because a sharp join between two textures is not visible. In band 2 the join is a ramp about ten pixels wide, in band 4 about forty. Each row shares one scale, so the third picture is the sum of the first two.

Part 2.4 · My own blends

Straight seam, my own photos: half a pair of glasses. The two photos from the hybrid section, with and without the mirrored glasses, registered and joined down the bridge of the nose with a step mask. This is what the method is made for: two pictures that agree everywhere except in one thing. In the cut the join shows anyway: the wall changes brightness at the seam, because the phone exposed each shot slightly differently, and the forehead does not quite match. The blend spreads the exposure difference over a wide band in the coarse levels, where it cannot be seen, and joins skin and hair over a few pixels in the fine ones. The glasses still end abruptly at the nose, as they must: there is nothing on the other side to blend into.

Irregular mask: a cat with a tiger's face. The tiger is aligned to the cat by the eyes, and the mask is an ellipse over the face, wide enough to take in the tiger's white cheeks, since pale fur next to pale fur is an easier join than orange next to gray. With the mask alone the result is a sticker. With the blend, whiskers and hairs stay sharp, and the orange drains into the tabby's beige with no edge to find. The images are 1200 × 1000, so the stack starts at σ = 2.

The process, as in figure 10 of the paper. Bands 0, 2 and 4 of each masked input and their sum, then all six bands added up. In band 0 the ellipse has a hard edge. By band 4 the tiger is a soft orange glow with no outline, which is why the join cannot be found.

A harder straight seam: Earth and Mars. My photos agree everywhere but in one place. These two agree nowhere: cloud against smooth ground, opposite colours. The discs are found by thresholding and matched in centre and radius. Now the number of levels matters. With four, the colour changes over a short distance and it still reads as a cut. With eight, the colours mix across most of the disc: Mars turns pink and the oceans go mauve. Six is the compromise. The blend hides a seam between pictures that are already similar at coarse scales; where they are not, the coarse bands have to change somewhere, and the only choice is over what distance.

A second irregular mask: yin and yang. The same planets joined along a curve, with a dot of each inside the other. A stack handles a seam in any direction, since the mask is only an image that gets blurred. The dots are 100 pixels across. With five levels (widest σ = 16) they hold. With six (σ = 32) the dot's mask is blurred almost flat in the coarse bands, so the dot keeps its texture but takes its colour from the planet around it, and fades. A region can only keep the bands that are narrower than it is.

Colour in blendingBells & whistles · part 2.4

Colour. I blend the three colour channels separately with the same mask stack. The blend is linear, so this is not really a choice: the gray of the colour blend is the blend of the grays (they agree to 9e-12). The real choice is whether colour should change over the same distance as brightness. I split each picture into luma and chroma, blended them with mask stacks of different widths, and added them back. On the oraple the versions are hard to tell apart, since the fruits are close in colour, so the experiment is on Earth and Mars.

In gray the join is still easy to find: cloud texture against smooth ground, so colour is not the only thing giving it away. A wider chroma mask spreads a pink haze onto Earth and pales Mars; a narrower one keeps both colours clean. I prefer the narrower: when two colours are this far apart, every mixture belongs to neither planet, so less mixture looks better. The last picture keeps the blended brightness and takes all its colour from Earth, and it reads as one planet. So colour is what says "two objects", and the way to use colour to enhance a blend is to make the colours agree, as they nearly do for the apple and the orange.

conv→multspace to frequency

The most important thing I learned

The equivalence of convolution and the Fourier transform: convolving an image with a filter is the same as multiplying their spectra. So I can use the frequency model of an image without ever computing its Fourier transform, just by convolving with well-known low-pass and high-pass filters.
