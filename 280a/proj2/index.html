Fun with filters and frequencies

Everything on this page is built from convolution. I write it by hand first, then use it to find edges and their directions, sharpen photos, make hybrid images that change with viewing distance, and blend two pictures without a visible seam.

Part 1.1 · Convolution from scratch

Convolution slides a small kernel over the image, and each output pixel is the dot product of the flipped kernel with the window under it. If the weights sum to 1, like the box filter's, the result is a moving average. Without padding, an n×n image and a k×k filter give an (n−k+1)×(n−k+1) output, so I pad the image with zeros first: k//2 before and (k−1)//2 after for same, and k−1 on every side for full.

def pad_zero(im, kh, kw, mode):
    if mode == "full":
        top, bottom, left, right = kh - 1, kh - 1, kw - 1, kw - 1
    elif mode == "same":
        top, bottom, left, right = kh // 2, (kh - 1) // 2, kw // 2, (kw - 1) // 2
    H, W = im.shape
    out = np.zeros((H + top + bottom, W + left + right))
    out[top:top + H, left:left + W] = im
    return out

Four loops. Two loops go over the output pixels and two over the kernel taps.

def conv2d_four(im, kernel, mode="same"):
    kh, kw = kernel.shape
    k = kernel[::-1, ::-1]                      # convolution flips the filter
    p = pad_zero(im, kh, kw, mode)
    H, W = p.shape[0] - kh + 1, p.shape[1] - kw + 1
    out = np.zeros((H, W))
    for i in range(H):
        for j in range(W):
            acc = 0.0
            for u in range(kh):
                for v in range(kw):
                    acc += k[u, v] * p[i + u, j + v]
            out[i, j] = acc
    return out

Two loops. The inner two loops collapse into one NumPy operation that multiplies the kernel by the window and sums the result.

    for i in range(H):
        for j in range(W):
            out[i, j] = np.sum(k * p[i:i + kh, j:j + kw])

Against scipy. Both versions match scipy.signal.convolve2d to rounding error in both modes. The box filter is symmetric, so it would not catch a missing flip. I also tested a 3×4 kernel holding the numbers 0 to 11, where the difference is 1e-14 with the flip and 17.5 without it.

Runtime. For the 81-tap box filter, two loops are 8 times faster than four. For the 2-tap derivative filters they are slower, because the fixed cost of a NumPy call is more than two multiplications. scipy is 20 to 200 times faster than either.

Boundaries. Zero padding darkens the border. After the box filter the top row averages 0.19, while the row five pixels in averages 0.34, which leaves a dark frame 4 pixels wide. scipy with zero fill does the same thing. The derivative filters pick that frame up as an edge, so from part 1.2 on I mirror the border instead.

Derivatives are drawn as 0.5 + 2 × value, so gray is zero. Dx picks up the pole and the sides of the glasses, Dy the brow and the moustache.

0.10→0.30threshold

Part 1.2 · Finite difference operator

Gradient magnitude. I get the partial derivatives by convolving with Dx = [1 −1] and its transpose Dy, so each output pixel is the difference between a pixel and its neighbour. The gradient magnitude √(gx² + gy²) measures how fast the brightness changes in any direction, and edges are the places where it is large.

gx, gy = conv(im, DX), conv(im, DY)
mag = np.sqrt(gx * gx + gy * gy)
edges = mag > threshold

Input. The course PNG has a white margin (7 rows on top, 1 below, 1 column on the left, 5 on the right). It showed up as the strongest edge in the picture, so I crop it off.

Threshold. I went with 0.20. At 0.10 and 0.15 the grass turns into speckle, because a difference of two pixels can't tell texture apart from an edge. At 0.25 and 0.30 the buildings and the far tripod leg start to break up. 0.20 keeps the man, the camera, the tripod and the skyline, and I accepted a little speckle in the grass to keep them.

2→1convolutions

Part 1.3 · Derivative of Gaussian filter

A Gaussian is a low-pass filter. It takes a weighted average of neighbouring pixels, which blurs the image. The speckle is high-frequency and taking differences amplifies it, so I blur before differentiating. I get the 1D kernel from cv2.getGaussianKernel with σ = 2 and width 6σ + 1 = 13, and take its outer product with itself to make it 2D.

g = cv2.getGaussianKernel(13, 2.0)     # 13 x 1
G = g @ g.T                            # 13 x 13
blurred = conv(im, G)
gx, gy = conv(blurred, DX), conv(blurred, DY)

What is different. The grass speckle is gone at every threshold. The edges are thicker and continuous, because blurring turns each edge into a ramp several pixels wide. Some detail is lost in exchange, and the small parts of the camera merge together. The largest magnitude drops from 0.87 to 0.16, since each step is now spread over more pixels, so the threshold has to drop too. 0.05 keeps the buildings and 0.07 loses them, so I picked 0.05.

One convolution. Because convolution is associative, (image ∗ G) ∗ Dx = image ∗ (G ∗ Dx), I can convolve G with Dx and Dy once. The results are derivative of Gaussian filters that blur and differentiate in a single pass.

dog_x = convolve2d(G, DX, mode="full")     # 13 x 14
dog_y = convolve2d(G, DY, mode="full")     # 14 x 13
gx, gy = conv(im, dog_x), conv(im, dog_y)

Same result. Each DoG filter looks like a positive bump next to a negative dip. The one-pass and two-pass results differ by at most 1e-15, and the edge maps agree on every pixel at all five thresholds.

0→360degrees

Gradient orientationBells & whistles · part 1

I put the gradient direction in the hue channel, since hue wraps around the same way angles do, and the magnitude in the value channel so flat regions stay dark. Saturation is fixed at 1.

The angle without an angle function. I can't use arctan2 or np.angle, so I use CORDIC. First I flip the vector into the right half plane. Then I rotate it onto the x axis in 24 steps of atan(2−i), turning clockwise or anticlockwise at each step depending on which one shrinks y. The angle is the signed sum of the steps. The step angles themselves come from the arctangent power series, so no library angle function appears anywhere.

CORDIC_ANGLES = [pi / 4] + [atan_series(2.0 ** -i) for i in range(1, 24)]

def orientation(gx, gy):
    x, y = gx.copy(), gy.copy()
    left = x < 0                                # fold into the right half plane
    x[left], y[left] = -x[left], -y[left]
    theta = np.zeros_like(x)
    for i, a in enumerate(CORDIC_ANGLES):
        d = np.where(y >= 0, 1.0, -1.0)         # which way shrinks y
        x, y = x + d * y * 2.0 ** -i, y - d * x * 2.0 ** -i
        theta += d * a
    theta[left] += pi
    return np.mod(theta, 2 * pi)

Compared with np.arctan2, the largest difference is 1.2e-07 radians. I also wrote the HSV to RGB conversion by hand, and it matches colorsys to 1e-16.

Reading it. Red means the gradient points right (dark to bright), yellow-green means up, cyan left and purple down. The coat's left edge is cyan because the bright sky is to its left. Each tripod leg shows up as two lines in opposite colours, one for each side. In the second picture everything is at full brightness, and the flat regions just show the direction of the noise. That is why the magnitude goes in the value channel.

0.5→4α

Part 2.1 · Image sharpening

Unsharp mask. A Gaussian blur keeps only the low frequencies, so subtracting the blurred image from the original leaves the high frequencies, which are the edges and fine texture. Adding those back scaled by α makes them stronger, and the image looks sharper.

One filter. sharpened = image + α (image − image ∗ G) = image ∗ ((1 + α) δ − α G), where δ is the unit impulse. The weights sum to 1, so flat regions keep their brightness.

def unsharp_kernel(sigma, alpha):
    g = cv2.getGaussianKernel(2 * ceil(3 * sigma) + 1, sigma)
    k = -alpha * (g @ g.T)
    k[k.shape[0] // 2, k.shape[1] // 2] += 1 + alpha
    return k

sharpened = convolve2d(channel, unsharp_kernel(sigma, alpha), mode="same", boundary="symm")

The single filter and the three separate steps agree to 1e-15. For colour images I filter each channel separately.

Varying α. At 0.5 and 1 the Taj Mahal just looks better focused. At 2 the dome gets a dark outline, and at 4 it has a halo and the JPEG blocks in the sky become visible. The halo comes from the negative weights around the centre of the filter. The captions give the fraction of values pushed outside [0, 1]. Those get clipped, so that detail is lost rather than sharpened.

My own picture. A wall I photographed for Project 0, with brushed concrete, lettering and palm fronds. The photo is 1000 pixels tall, so I use σ = 2.

A blurry picture. This selfie from Project 0 is soft because I used digital zoom. At α = 2 the curls separate and the glasses get their edges back. There are no halos, because a soft picture has little high-frequency content to overshoot with.

Evaluation: blur, then sharpen. I blurred the sharp wall with σ = 2 and then sharpened it with the same σ. The lettering comes back with steep edges, but the brush strokes in the concrete are gone and sharpening does not bring them back.

High-frequency energy (the RMS of the picture minus its blur) is 0.047 in the original, 0.010 after blurring, and back up to 0.045 at α = 8. The RMS error against the original, though, only improves from 0.047 to 0.040 at best (α = 2), and at α = 8 it is 0.061, worse than not sharpening at all. The blur multiplied the finest frequencies by almost zero, and the unsharp mask multiplies them by at most 1 + α, which still leaves them near zero. Sharpening steepens the edges that survived the blur, but it can't restore detail the blur removed.

Part 2.2 · Hybrid images

A hybrid image is the high-pass of one picture added to the low-pass of another. Up close the fine detail dominates what you see. From far away the eye can't resolve it, only the low frequencies are left, and the picture turns into the other one.

Alignment. The two pictures have to line up where it matters, or you just see two things on top of each other. I stored two points per picture (the eyes) in a file instead of clicking them each time, and compute the similarity transform that moves the cat's eyes onto Derek's.

t = SimilarityTransform.from_estimate(cat_eyes, derek_eyes)
cat_aligned = warp(cat, t.inverse, output_shape=derek.shape[:2], mode="edge", order=3)

The filters. The low-pass is a Gaussian. The high-pass is the impulse minus a Gaussian, which is the same as subtracting the blurred picture from the original.

def hybrid(low_im, high_im, s_low, s_high):
    low = blur(low_im, s_low)
    high = high_im - blur(high_im, s_high)      # impulse minus Gaussian
    return np.clip(low + high, 0, 1)

Choosing the cutoffs. I take the cutoff of a Gaussian of width σ to be the frequency where it passes half the amplitude, f = √(ln 2 / 2) / (πσ) cycles per pixel. In the grid below, the low-pass σ increases going down and the high-pass σ increases going across. With a low-pass σ of 4, Derek keeps his eyes and teeth and competes with the cat up close. At 16 he is a blob even from far away. With a high-pass σ of 6 the cat is only whiskers, and at 24 the cat is still visible from far away. I chose σ = 9 for Derek (0.021 cycles per pixel) and σ = 14 for the cat (0.013). The two bands overlap a little, which is better than leaving a gap between them where neither picture reads.

In the frequency domain. These are log |FFT| images with zero frequency in the centre. Derek's spectrum falls off quickly, since he is a smooth face on a plain background. The cat's is broader because of the fur, and it has diagonal streaks from the shelf and the wall. The low-pass keeps a small disc in the centre and the high-pass keeps everything except the centre, so the hybrid's spectrum is the first one sitting inside the second. The thin cross along the axes comes from the picture's border. The FFT treats the picture as if it repeats, so the jump from one side to the opposite side acts as an edge, and blurring inside the picture doesn't remove it.

Colour in hybrid imagesBells & whistles · part 2.2

Colour helps in the low frequencies. I split each picture into gray and chroma and measured how much of the chroma energy survives each filter. The low-pass keeps 93% of Derek's, and the high-pass keeps only 4% of the cat's. Colour mostly changes slowly across whole regions, and a high-pass throws regions away. So putting colour in the high-frequency picture gives almost the same result as the gray hybrid, while colour in the low gives Derek his skin tone and makes him easier to pick out from far away. Colour in both looks the same as colour in the low alone, so I use both.

Part 2.3 · Gaussian and Laplacian stacks

A pyramid blurs and halves the picture at each level, while a stack blurs and keeps the full size. Level 0 of my Gaussian stack is the picture itself, and each later level is the previous one blurred again, with σ = 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16, for 6 levels in total. Doubling σ at each level plays the role of halving the size. I don't use any pyramid functions; the only library call is the Gaussian blur.

Laplacian stack. Each level is the difference between two neighbouring Gaussian levels, which is the band of frequencies the next blur removed. The last level is just the last Gaussian level. The differences telescope, so adding up the stack gives back the picture (to 1e-16). Blending depends on this, since it changes each band separately and then adds them back up.

def gaussian_stack(im, levels, sigma0=1.0):
    out = [im]
    for k in range(1, levels):
        out.append(blur(out[-1], sigma0 * 2 ** (k - 1)))
    return out

def laplacian_stack(im, levels, sigma0=1.0):
    g = gaussian_stack(im, levels, sigma0)
    return [g[k] - g[k + 1] for k in range(levels - 1)] + [g[-1]]

Laplacian levels are drawn with gray as zero, each stretched to its own range. The last level is a Gaussian level, drawn as it is.

Part 2.4 · Multiresolution blending

A straight cut leaves a visible seam because the join is abrupt at every frequency. Feathering the mask with a single width doesn't fix it. A narrow feather still leaves a step in colour, and a wide one makes the textures ghost. Burt and Adelson use a different width for each frequency band. I blend each band of the Laplacian stack with its own mask width, so fine detail is joined over a few pixels and broad colour over about a quarter of the picture.

I follow the algorithm on page 230 of the paper. The mask is a step function that is 1 on the apple's side. Its Gaussian stack uses the same σ values as the pictures' stacks, so level k of the mask is blurred to the scale of band k. Each band is mask × apple + (1 − mask) × orange, and the result is the sum of the bands.

def blend(a, b, mask, levels=6):
    la, lb = laplacian_stack(a, levels), laplacian_stack(b, levels)
    gm = gaussian_stack(mask, levels)
    bands = [gm[k] * la[k] + (1 - gm[k]) * lb[k] for k in range(levels)]
    return np.clip(sum(bands), 0, 1)

The Gaussian stack of the mask. Mk weights band k: the finest band is joined along a hard line and the coarsest over a wide ramp.

The figure. This recreates Figure 3.42 of Szeliski with my stacks: bands 0, 2 and 4, then the sum of all bands. In band 0 the halves meet along a sharp line. That is fine, because a sharp join between two fine textures isn't noticeable. In band 2 the join is a ramp about ten pixels wide, and in band 4 it is about forty. The three pictures in each row are drawn at the same scale, so the third one is the sum of the first two.

Part 2.4 · My own blends

Straight seam with my own photos: half a pair of glasses. These are the two registered photos from the hybrid section, with and without the mirrored glasses, joined down the bridge of the nose with a step mask. The method works best on pairs like this, which match everywhere except for one thing. Even so, the plain cut shows a seam. The phone exposed the two shots slightly differently, so the wall changes brightness at the seam, and the forehead doesn't quite match either. The blend spreads the exposure difference across a wide band in the coarse levels, where you can't see it, and joins the skin and hair over a few pixels in the fine levels. The glasses still end abruptly at the nose, which is expected, since there is nothing on the other side for them to blend into.

Straight seam: the Golden Gate, night to day. Two photos taken from Battery Spencer, the viewpoint above the north tower, by two different photographers at different times of day. The framing is close but not identical, so I register the night photo onto the day one using two points, the top of the tower and the deck at its base. I put the seam down the middle of the tower, since that is the part that has to line up. In the blend the sky goes from dusk to noon across the picture, the city lights sit under the daytime skyline, and the tower is lit orange on one side and red on the other without a visible join. The blend can't hide where the two photos disagree, though. The decks leave the tower at slightly different angles, because the two cameras weren't in exactly the same spot.

A harder straight seam: Earth and Mars. The glasses photos matched everywhere except in one place. Earth and Mars don't match anywhere: Earth has clouds where Mars has smooth ground, and their colours are opposite. I find each disc by thresholding and match their centres and radii. Here the number of levels makes a big difference. With four levels the colour changes over a short distance and the join still looks like a cut. With eight, the colours mix across most of the disc, so Mars turns pink and the oceans go mauve. I went with six as a middle ground. Blending can hide a seam when the two pictures are already similar at coarse scales. When they aren't, the coarse bands have to change somewhere, and the number of levels only decides over what distance that happens.

Irregular mask: yin and yang. The same two planets joined along a curve, with a dot of each inside the other. A stack handles a seam in any direction, since the mask is only an image that gets blurred. The dots are 100 pixels across. With five levels (widest σ = 16) they hold. With six (σ = 32) the dot's mask is blurred almost flat in the coarse bands, so the dot keeps its texture but takes its colour from the planet around it, and fades. A region can only keep the bands that are narrower than it is. This is my favourite result, so its bands are laid out below as in figure 10 of the paper.

The process, as in figure 10 of the paper. Bands 0, 2 and 4 of each masked input and their sum, then all five bands added up. In band 0 the curve and the dots have hard edges. By band 4 each planet is a soft glow with no outline, and the dots have all but gone, which is why one more level makes them fade.

Colour in blendingBells & whistles · part 2.4

I blend the three colour channels separately with the same mask stack. Because the blend is linear, converting the colour blend to gray gives the same result as blending the gray images (they agree to 9e-12), so blending in colour does not change the brightness. The more useful question is whether colour should change over the same distance as brightness. To test this I split each picture into luma and chroma, blended them with mask stacks of different widths, and added them back together. On the oraple the versions are hard to tell apart because the two fruits are close in colour, so I ran the experiment on Earth and Mars.

Even in gray the join is easy to find, since cloud texture meets smooth ground, so colour isn't the only thing giving it away. A wider chroma mask spreads a pink haze over Earth and makes Mars paler, while a narrower one keeps both colours clean. I prefer the narrower one. When two colours are this far apart, any mixture of them looks like neither planet, so less mixing looks better. The last picture keeps the blended brightness but takes all its colour from Earth, and it reads as one planet. Most of what makes Earth and Mars look like two objects is the colour difference, so the way to use colour to improve a blend is to make the colours agree, as they nearly do for the apple and the orange.

conv→multspace to frequency

The most important thing I learned

The convolution theorem: convolving an image with a filter is the same as multiplying their spectra. So I can use the frequency model of an image without ever computing its Fourier transform, just by convolving with well-known low-pass and high-pass filters.
